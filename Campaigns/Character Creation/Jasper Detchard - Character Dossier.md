# JASPER DETCHARD — Complete Character Dossier
## For D&D Adaptation Reference
### Source: *The Henchmen of Zenda* by KJ Charles (2018)

---

## I. IDENTITY & PHYSICAL DESCRIPTION

**Full Name:** Jasper Detchard (self-chosen; birth name abandoned and never revealed)

**Age:** 36 at the start of the story; 60 at the time of writing his memoirs.

**Nationality:** English (self-exiled; has not returned to England)

**Physical Appearance** (from Rassendyll's description, quoted by Detchard):
> "The Englishman, Detchard, a narrow-faced fellow, with close-cut fair hair and a bronzed complexion. He was a finely made man, broad in the shoulder and slender in the hips. A good fighter, but a crooked customer."
— Ch. 1

Later self-descriptions add:
> "I dare say I looked intimidating, all scars and sinew."
— Ch. 14

> "I bowed, something I do with a great deal less grace than Hentzau."
— Ch. 15

By the epilogue: "old and grizzled" at sixty, still active.

**Physical Summary:** Tall (a couple of inches taller than Hentzau), narrow-faced, fair-haired, sun-bronzed skin. Broad-shouldered, lean-hipped, sinewy rather than bulky. Covered in scars. Heavy hands. Intimidating presence. Moves with a soldier's economy rather than an aristocrat's grace.

## II. ORIGIN & BACKSTORY

### The Name
Detchard chose his own identity after being disowned:

> "My name is not Jasper Detchard. Obviously it is not. I had another name once, but I stained it at school with various crimes, culminating in a sufficiently serious indiscretion that my parents declined to give me the opportunity of being thrown out of a university too, and disposed of me via the Army and a posting to India. [...] I was cashiered. I left the Army with numerous prizes for swordplay, a dishonourable discharge, a reputation for cheating at cards (fairly earned), another for indulgence in the fuck that dare not speak its name (ditto), and a request from my family that I no longer claim kinship with them, since they wanted none with me. I did them that service and **named myself Detchard, after the first town where I sucked a man off, and Jasper, because if one's lot in life is to be a Victorian villain, one might as well play the game properly.**"
— Ch. 1

**Analysis:** His name is an act of defiant self-creation. He chose an identity that encodes his sexuality and his role as a "villain" — embracing both rather than hiding either. This is foundational to his character: he is, uniquely among the cast, "entirely his own creation" (Thematic Matrix).

### Class Background
Upper-class English: public school (Rugby), destined for university, family wealth.

> "My hand bore a reasonable resemblance to Rassendyll's; I suppose we had both had the same public-school training."
— Ch. 16

> "I had spent most of my life in boarding school, the Army, and the more insalubrious back streets of half a dozen cities."
— Ch. 14

Despite his origins, he lives in servants' quarters at Zenda while the nobleman Hentzau gets the guest rooms. He is acutely aware of class hierarchy and contemptuous of inherited privilege:

> "If you'd been born with two thousand pounds a year and an inclination for the fair sex, I dare say you'd never have broken a law in your life."
— Ch. 13 (to Hentzau)

> "Greater men sit on a throne and order tortures; I simply red my hands on their behalf."
— Ch. 17

### Military Service
British Army, posted to India. Won prizes for swordplay. Cashiered (dishonourably discharged) for unspecified crimes — implied to include homosexuality and cheating at cards.

### The Demimonde Life
After the Army, Detchard reinvented himself as a Continental mercenary, adventurer, and gambler:

> "So there I was, rootless, wandering, rebaptised into a new life without family or history. I worked my way back from India using my cards, wits, and blade, but did not trouble England's shores. The Continent is a more welcoming place for my sort of people: adventurers, wanderers, gentleman thieves and gentleman thugs, ladies of the day and night, gamblers and fences and information brokers, aristocrats brought low, masquerading commoners reaching dangerously high. We of that tribe drift in and out of lives and languages and countries, a loose fraternity of rogues with a looser tangle of loyalties and rivalries, and one may see the same face over the course of years in a Berlin nightspot, a Prague palace, and a Roman gaol."
— Ch. 1

### Meeting Antoinette
Their friendship is the relationship that sets the entire plot in motion:

> "I was in Vienna when I first met a very pretty Frenchwoman of seventeen flaunted as mistress by a dishonest stockbroker three times her age. I next encountered her when I was hired as bravo at an extremely elegant and accommodating brothel in Dresden where she presided over the festivities. [...] Three years later, when I lay in a Paris gaol cell awaiting the inevitable discovery of a single, deadly proof of my guilt and the equally inevitable trip to Madame Guillotine, it was my pretty Frenchwoman to whom I wrote in desperation, and she who bailed and bribed me out, at eye-watering cost."
— Ch. 1

> "Not lovers, if you mean that, but she saved my neck from the guillotine once. She is dear to me."
— Ch. 13

### Previous Lover: Henri
> "Henri? We parted. He is studying to become a priest, I hear. [...] He said it was all or nothing, and since I couldn't give him anything like all, we settled for nothing."
— Ch. 9

> "Henri was a good soul with an unfortunate taste for bad men, and he deserved happiness, but people rarely get what they deserve. He had wanted far more than I had to give, and I could not resent him for leaving me. [...] Rootless adventurers bound for a bad end have no right to dally with serious men. It wasn't fair of me."
— Ch. 9

> "And I had not done well by my last lover: I did not, in truth, deserve peaceful embraces."
— Ch. 14

**Analysis:** Henri is the ghost of Detchard's emotional limitations. He acknowledges the failure was his own — he *couldn't* give enough, not that Henri asked too much. This wound informs his entire approach to Hentzau: the fear that he will repeat the same failure.

## III. PERSONALITY & TEMPERAMENT

### Core Traits

**Pragmatic to the bone.** Detchard's defining characteristic is ruthless practicality. He does what works, not what's noble.

**Emotionally guarded.** He presents as cold and unsentimental but is repeatedly revealed to feel deeply:
> "But when I care for anyone, friend or lover — and that is not often — I care deeply. True companionship is a rare prize in a life like mine, where most people are, of necessity, disposable."
— Ch. 11

**Self-aware.** He knows his own flaws and names them:
> "Conscience doth make cowards of us all, or so they say; I've never troubled much with it myself."
— Ch. 13

Yet his actions consistently contradict his self-image. He is uncomfortable with the imprisonment of Rudolf:
> "I have no desire to think of him after those endless days guarding his cell, listening to his shouts and orders degenerate into pleas and muttering. It was an unclean business."
— Ch. 17

**Sardonic and literary.** His voice blends classical education (Catullus, Shakespeare, Webster) with barracks profanity and bone-dry understatement:
> "What a pile of shit."
— Ch. 1 (his opening line, on Rassendyll's memoir)

> "He'd had too many State dinners, I concluded."
— Ch. 17 (mid-swordfight with Rassendyll)

> "a quadruple agent by now, and I could not hold back a laugh at the thought."
— Ch. 16

> "two murderers in the process of committing treason at a funeral, and I can only say, that is what Ruritania and Rupert Hentzau do to a man."
— Ch. 15

**Fatalistic but not passive.** He accepts that his life could end at any moment but does not stop fighting:
> "I haven't died yet, and I don't intend to start now."
— Ch. 16

### The Mask vs. The Man

Detchard insists he is amoral, heartless, and incapable of sentiment. The text systematically undermines every one of these claims:

| What He Claims | What He Does |
|:---|:---|
| "Morals are an inconvenience" | Objects to Rudolf's squalid imprisonment; calls it "unclean" |
| "I am not a sentimental man" | Risks everything for Antoinette; turns back for Hentzau in the moat |
| Claims no conscience | Feels guilt about Henri; discomfort at unnecessary killings |
| "I don't give a fuck for politics" | Helps install Flavia because she keeps promises and Michael doesn't |
| Frames everything as transactional | Attends Antoinette's wedding; teaches Lisl to use a knife; travels with Hentzau for 20+ years |

Antoinette's assessment captures this perfectly:
> "You have plenty of heart, just no soul."
— Ch. 3 (attributed)

## IV. MORAL CODE & WORLDVIEW

### Core Values
1. **Friendship above everything.**
   > "I don't give a fuck for politics. I am here — I have always been here — to help my friend."
   — Ch. 13

   > "I put friendship above country, a very long way. Ruritania's affairs are not my business."
   — Ch. 15

   > "One must help one's companions in life, or what else is there?"
   — Ch. 18

2. **No loyalty to institutions.** He serves no crown, nation, or cause. Only people.
   > "I am willing to act for Antoinette. I'm not one for service."
   — Ch. 15

3. **Competence over breeding.** He judges people by what they can do, not what they were born to.

4. **Clean kills over cruelty.** He prefers swift ends:
   > "Had it been up to me I should have given him a swift death weeks before."
   — Ch. 17 (on Rudolf's imprisonment)

5. **Honesty (in private).** He values lack of hypocrisy:
   > "Hentzau, who had almost every possible fault but was at least no hypocrite."
   — Ch. 13

6. **Embrace weakness as strength.**
   > "Embrace the insults, Hentzau. Let people believe they know your weakness, and they will be weaker for it. Personally, I like nothing more than an opponent who suggests I am less of a fighter because of who I fuck. That tends to make them delightfully overconfident."
   — Ch. 13

### On Love
> "Love is best avoided altogether; it is rarely anything but a bloody nuisance."
— Thematic Matrix (attributed quote)

Yet every significant action he takes in the story is motivated by love — for Antoinette, for Hentzau, for companionship itself.

## V. COMBAT ABILITIES & FIGHTING STYLE

### Weapons Proficiency
Detchard is arguably the most competent fighter in the book. His arsenal before the final battle:
> "I checked my weapons over and made sure I had my sword to hand, my belt knife, my concealed knife at my back, my boot knife, and a loaded revolver. I don't like to be unprepared."
— Ch. 17

His preferred weapon:
> "I prefer the knife."
— Ch. 16

### Swordsmanship
Won "numerous prizes for swordplay" in the Army. His style is pragmatic rather than elegant:
- Beats Hentzau in their first practice bout "by brute pragmatism rather than elegance" (Ch. 4)
- Serves as Hentzau's sword instructor — "He had learned well from my teaching" (Ch. 17)
- Can control the pace of a fight while *appearing* to lose: "I upped the tempo of my own movements, pressing him harder but not too hard, needing to conceal that I wasn't trying to finish him." (Ch. 17)
- When fighting for real: "I attacked without reserve, sending Rassendyll staggering back and stumbling under my onslaught" (Ch. 17)

### Tactical Mind
- Reads opponents psychologically: "Hentzau pricking and poking to drive his opponent into reckless anger. He struck at recklessness in other men because he knew it to be his own weakness" (Ch. 4)
- Pre-battle discipline: "I made sure I slept, and ate well, and exercised lightly to keep my muscles warm." (Ch. 17)
- Always has contingency plans: "I had also left a small bag of necessities at a certain inn (three in fact; I like to hedge my bets)." (Ch. 18)

### Combat Reflexes
> "I seized it and was close to breaking the wrist before I realised my assailant was Antoinette."
— Ch. 14

### Fighting as Art
The staged bridge duel with Hentzau reveals his deepest feelings about combat:
> "Nobody interrupted us; nobody fired a gun. The watchers stood rapt as we fought in savage silence for those few, brief perfect moments, bathed in shadow and moonlight on the bridge: hero, villain, henchman, lover."
— Ch. 17

> "It felt as though I could read his mind, as though one intelligence choreographed us both in perfect harmony. This was better than fucking, I thought, and would have liked to shout that aloud and watch him laugh."
— Ch. 17

## VI. SKILLS & TRADECRAFT

### Espionage & Deception
- Successfully maintained cover as Michael's loyal henchman while secretly working for Antoinette and then Flavia — Michael, despite constant surveillance, "never breaks it" (Relationship Map)
- Staged intimate encounters with Hentzau to neutralize Michael's suspicions via spyholes (Ch. 14)
- Forged Rassendyll's handwriting convincingly: "My hand bore a reasonable resemblance to Rassendyll's" (Ch. 16)

### Professional Preparedness
- Default demeanour under pressure: "grimly uncommunicative, a demeanour I could carry off well thanks to years of practice" (Ch. 14)
- Always armed: concealed knives at multiple locations on his body
- Stashed escape funds across the border at multiple locations (Ch. 18)
- Fluent in the demimonde's codes and signals: knows brothel warning signals, card-cheating techniques

### Other Skills
- Card sharping (self-admitted: "fairly earned" reputation)
- Languages (implied fluency across Continental Europe)
- Teaching: instructs Hentzau in swordsmanship; teaches Lisl to "fuzz cards and use a knife"
- Classical education: reads Catullus in the original Latin, quotes Shakespeare and Webster

## VII. KEY RELATIONSHIPS

### Antoinette de Mauban — "My Friend"
The bedrock relationship. She saved his life; he came to Zenda to repay the debt. Their bond is the most stable in the book:

> "She is dear to me."
— Ch. 13

> "You deserve a man who adores you. Find one, and we'll make sure he knows that if he lays a hand on you, I shall break his kneecaps with a hammer."
— Ch. 9

> "I'm sorry I couldn't do better." / "You've done all you could. You've been a pal, mon cher."
— Ch. 16

Post-story: He attends her wedding, teaches her daughter survival skills, maintains the friendship for life.

**D&D Note:** This is a bond of absolute, unconditional loyalty that predates the story and survives everything. It's the kind of bond that would be a character's most important feature on a backstory sheet.

### Rupert of Hentzau — "My Rupert, My Glorious Villain"
The central romance. Begins as physical attraction, deepens into genuine companionship despite Detchard's resistance:

**The Attraction:**
> "It wasn't that he was a beauty. That catches my interest but doesn't keep it. It was the pure unrestrained, unashamed, bursting life of him, the way he flung himself into whatever he did with utter commitment. For most of us, living is simply our ongoing state until we die; Rupert lived as an active verb, and did it as hard as he could."
— Ch. 14

**The Realisation:**
> "Rupert of Hentzau was a beauty, a devil, a glorious fuck, a sharp wit, and all that was enjoyable, but I had not quite realised before this moment that I had come to regard him too as a companion."
— Ch. 11

**The Commitment (such as it is):**
> "I don't need fidelity, or adoration, or extravagant vows. Those are all will-o'-the-wisps. Whereas good manners require effort."
— Ch. 15

> "I claim no right over your affections. You may do as you please [...] and so shall I. But I appreciate the courtesy."
— Ch. 15

**The Depth (revealed under pressure):**
> "I wanted to say the hell with it all, to stand side by side on the bridge with my Rupert, my glorious villain, and take on any man who dared oppose us, and it took all my will to keep control."
— Ch. 17

**The Aftermath:**
> "We adventured. We roamed Europe for a year and more, fucking and fighting as we pleased; we parted when our wanderlust took us in different directions, and met again in new cities."
— Ch. 18

They are still together 20+ years later. Hentzau reads over Detchard's shoulder as he writes.

### The No-Kissing Rule
A physical embodiment of his emotional self-censorship. Detchard will have sex with men but will not kiss them — maintaining a barrier of intimacy. The rule's gradual erosion with Hentzau tracks his emotional arc:
- First broken on the hillside (Ch. 12) — their first genuine emotional intimacy
- Broken again tactically to communicate about spyholes (Ch. 14)
- Broken deliberately as farewell: "I pulled him to me, kissed him once, hard, and released him." (Ch. 16)

### Michael Elphberg — The Enemy
Coerced service masking genuine contempt:
> "If our plotting had been overheard, our lives would be forfeit. Well, that was always part of the game, but I intended to take Michael with me if I possibly could."
— Ch. 14

Detchard's performance of loyalty is his greatest act of tradecraft.

### Rudolf Rassendyll — Professional Contempt
> "It takes one to know one, you red-haired prick."
— Ch. 1

Rassendyll represents everything Detchard distrusts: "a dilettante playing at danger with the safety net of his class and accent, incapable of self-knowledge."

## VIII. CHARACTER ARC

### Beginning (Ch. 1)
Rootless, mercenary, owing a debt and intending to pay it. No expectation of being changed by the job. "I am reliably informed I lack a soul."

### Midpoint (Ch. 11)
Troubled by his growing affection for Hentzau; chooses to trust him anyway. Commits to Flavia's cause — not for politics, but for Antoinette.

### Climax (Ch. 17)
On the bridge, fighting Hentzau in moonlight, he experiences the most intense connection of his life. Then turns back from escape to rescue him from the moat — running toward danger "entirely out of ideas but knowing there was, in the end, nothing else I could do."

> "I am a mercenary, a henchman, cannon fodder for those whose battles make up the pages of history. Men like me skulk away into the darkness, or die in it. Heroic rescues are for heroes."
— Ch. 17

> "And with all that, I did not flee into the woods."
— Ch. 17

His actions contradict his self-image. He *is* capable of heroism; he simply refuses to call it that.

### End (Ch. 18)
Quietly at ease. Still a mercenary at sixty, still heading into danger — but not alone.

> "One must help one's companions in life, or what else is there?"
— Ch. 18

> "And it should, at least, be interesting."
— Ch. 18 (final line)

## IX. SELF-IDENTITY: "A HENCHMAN"

Detchard is deeply conscious of being a supporting character in someone else's story — and that awareness is both his wound and his freedom:

> "I am a mercenary, a henchman, cannon fodder for those whose battles make up the pages of history. Men like me skulk away into the darkness, or die in it."
— Ch. 17

> "I, a minor henchman, die ignominiously, slipping on the blood of my innocent victim."
— Ch. 18 (quoting Rassendyll's account of his "death")

> "I could almost find it in my heart to pity Rassendyll, you know."
— Ch. 18

He writes his memoirs explicitly to correct the record — to tell the henchman's story. The entire novel is his refusal to be written out.

**D&D Note:** This self-conception as "the guy who does the dirty work while heroes get the glory" is deeply gameable. He'd be the character who insists he's just in it for the money while consistently risking his life for the party.

## X. THEMATIC SIGNIFICANCE (for D&D flavour)

### Key Themes to Embody

1. **Loyalty to people, not causes.** He has no country, no king, no ideology. Only friends.
2. **Performance vs. authenticity.** He chose his name, his identity, his role. He is the only character who is entirely self-made.
3. **The lie of the "hero narrative."** He knows the bards will give credit to someone else. He does the work anyway.
4. **Love as vulnerability.** His emotional armour (the no-kissing rule, the cynicism, the transactional framing) is real armour against real danger — in a world where being queer can get you hanged, intimacy is genuinely risky.
5. **Competence as identity.** He defines himself by what he can do, not what he was born to.

## XI. D&D ADAPTATION NOTES

### Suggested Character Traits

**Personality Traits:**
- "I named myself. I chose who I am. That's more than most people can say."
- "For most of us, living is simply our ongoing state until we die. I prefer to make it interesting."

**Ideals:**
- Companionship: "One must help one's companions in life, or what else is there?"
- Freedom: "I'm not one for service."

**Bonds:**
- A lifelong friend (Antoinette equivalent) who saved his life and to whom he owes everything.
- A companion/lover who is everything he tells himself he doesn't need.

**Flaws:**
- Emotionally closed-off; pushes people away to avoid vulnerability.
- Cynicism tips into passivity — watches bad situations develop when earlier action might help.
- "I have never troubled much with [conscience] myself." (He has. He lies about it.)

### Key Contradictions to Play
- Claims to be amoral → has a strict personal code
- Claims not to care → turns back into danger for his companions every time
- Claims to be "just a henchman" → is arguably the most competent person in any room
- Claims love is a nuisance → every major decision is motivated by love
- Has a rule against kissing → breaks it for the right person

### Voice Notes
- Dry, sardonic, literary. Quotes poetry and follows it with profanity.
- Understatement is his default register. "It was a damned long ten miles to the border."
- Never explains himself when he can deflect with a joke.
- Addresses the reader (or listener) directly; aware he is telling a story.

### Combat Style for D&D
- Pragmatic over elegant; wins by experience and brutality, not flourish
- Prefers knives but excels with swords
- Always over-armed: multiple concealed weapons
- Pre-plans escape routes obsessively
- Can throw a fight convincingly (useful for deception-based play)
- Fighting alongside a trusted companion unlocks his best work
